The science behind OrbitKeep

How friendship
actually forms

OrbitKeep is built on research about time, attention, and intentional contact — then turned into a simple dashboard you can act on.

~50h casual friend
~90h friend
200h+ close friend
Hall’s hours to climb stages most-cited

Jeffrey A. Hall (2018/19) studied how many hours people spend together before they rate someone as a closer friend. The viral cutoffs (~50% chance of moving up a level):

~50hours
Acquaintance → casual friend
Often summarized as 40–60 hours. First real “we hang out” relationship, not just name recognition.
~90hours
Casual friend → friend
Often 80–100 hours. Someone you’d actively plan time with — not only see when convenient.
200+hours
Friend → close / good friend
One analysis put the ~50% transition near ~219 hours. Shared life, not occasional catch-ups.

KU’s summary of combined results: 40–60 → casual, 80–100 → friend, 200+ → good/close. Regression point estimates sometimes cited: ~94 / ~164 / ~219. Descriptive emergence can appear earlier (~30 / ~50 / ~140 / ~300 for best). The popular 50 / 90 / 200 is a clean average — not a rigid law.

How the study was done
Study 1 — 355 adults

People who had moved in the last six months named someone new they’d met (not family/romance/prior friend), estimated hours spent, and rated the relationship.

Study 2 — 112 KU freshmen

Followed over the first weeks of school as new acquaintances formed, with follow-ups as friendships crystallized.

Hall, J. A. — Journal of Social and Personal Relationships (online 2018). DOI: 10.1177/0265407518761225

What the research actually says
Time is necessary, not sufficient. Hundreds of hours with coworkers can still leave you “acquaintances” if you never socialize outside work or simply don’t click. Work co-presence counts less than leisure/social time.
What you do matters. Faster closeness linked to joking, meaningful conversation, catching up, and leisure — not just sharing a classroom or desk.
It’s mutual and voluntary. You can’t force the hours. Hall’s practical advice: invite people and change context (lunch, drinks, non-work hangouts) to signal “I want friendship.”
Speed of accumulation matters. People who climbed stages often doubled or tripled time over weeks — not drip-fed hours over years. Adult life after school/work stops forcing contact is exactly why friendship feels hard.
Limits of the data. US movers + college freshmen; self-reported hours; “friend” labels are subjective. Don’t treat 50/90/200 as universal across every culture or personality.
Dunbar’s layers counts, not hours

Robin Dunbar’s idea: we only have room for so many relationships at each depth. Hall quantified hours to form closeness; Dunbar limits how many bonds you can maintain.

What those hours look like
~50h · casual friend

≈ 2 hours/week for ~6 months, or a few intense weekends + weekly hangs.

~biweekly+
~90h · friend

≈ 2–3 hours/week for most of a year, or denser over a semester.

weekly-ish
200h+ · close friend

≈ 4 hours/week for a year — or a summer of shared life (travel, roommates, project, kids’ sports).

high density

Catch up only a couple of hours a month and close friendship can take years — which is why adult friendship feels hard once school and work stop forcing hours together.

How this maps to your dashboard

OrbitKeep does not track raw co-presence hours (Hall’s raw measure). It tracks what you can control: intentional contact cadence — the habit that accumulates quality time over months.

Core formulas
next_due = last_contact + frequency_days

Default frequency is 30 days. Logging an interaction sets last_contact (max-date rule) and recomputes next_due.

New person, no contact yet

When you log an interaction, you’re recording intentional time — joking, catching up, hanging out — the quality contact Hall found matters most. The dashboard’s job is simple: surface who needs that next quality hour before they drift out of orbit.
